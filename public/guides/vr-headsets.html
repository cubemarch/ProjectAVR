Next-gen VR headsets, compared

Last updated 3 November 2026 · prices and specs checked the same day

The short version. For most people watching VR video, the Meta Quest 3 is still the easy answer: sharp enough, wide enough, and every major VR video app runs on it. If you want the best picture money can buy and you have a gaming PC, look at the Bigscreen Beyond 2. And if you are curious about the new Meta VR Glasses arriving in spring 2027, they are lighter and have a richer picture than anything Meta has made, but a much narrower view, which matters a lot for 180° video. Details below.

First, the five numbers that matter

Spec sheets are long. For watching VR video, only a few lines change what you actually see. Here is what each one means, in plain words.

Resolution per eye

How many pixels each eye gets. More pixels means less of the "screen door" grid and finer detail in faces, hair and skin. Always compare the per eye figure; some brands quote both eyes added together to make the number look bigger.

Pixels per degree (PPD)

The honest sharpness number. It is the resolution spread across how wide the view is, so a headset with fewer pixels but a narrower view can look sharper than one with more. Around 20 is good, 25 is very good, and the micro-OLED headsets reach the mid-30s, where the picture starts to look like a screen rather than a grid.

A useful rule of thumb: an 8K 180° video gives each eye about 23 pixels per degree. A headset sharper than that will show you everything in the file; a softer one throws some of it away. That is why the same 8K file looks crisper on a Quest 3 than on a Quest 3S, and why the high-end headsets reward the highest-resolution versions a site offers.

Field of view (FOV)

How much of your vision the picture fills. This is the number people overlook, and it is the one that decides how immersive VR video feels. Most 180° video is shot to wrap well past your peripheral vision, so a headset around 100–110° horizontally feels like being there, while one at 70° feels more like looking through a large window. Neither is wrong, but they are different experiences.

Display type: LCD or micro-OLED

LCDs are bright and affordable but their blacks are really dark grey, which shows in dim scenes. Micro-OLED panels switch each pixel fully off, so blacks are truly black and colours pop. They are also tiny and dense, which is how the premium headsets get so sharp. The trade-off is cost, and usually a narrower view.

Weight, and where it sits

A film is longer than a game session. A headset that feels fine for twenty minutes can be a chore at ninety. Watch for the battery: some headsets move it to the back strap or into a pack on a cable, which balances the weight better than the headline figure suggests.

What it means for VR video

The good news is the picture. Early hands-on reports describe deep, true blacks with no smearing, colours a big step beyond Quest's LCDs, and the sharpest image Meta has shipped. For dark, moody scenes that is a real upgrade. And at 100 grams, a full-length film stops being a neck workout.

The catch is the 70° view. A 180° video will look gorgeous but framed, more like a giant window in front of you than a room around you. Meta is openly pitching this as a media and everyday device rather than a gaming one; it is already promoting 3D cinema releases for it. If being surrounded is the point for you, the Quest 3 is still the more immersive headset, at half the price.

Who it is for: people who want comfort and the richest picture for long viewing sessions, and do not mind trading some immersion for it.

Meta Quest 3 The safe pick

Three years on, still the headset most people should buy for VR video. It is wide (110°), sharp enough to do 8K files justice (about 25 pixels per degree), fully wireless, and every major VR video player (DeoVR, HereSphere, SKYBOX and the studios' own apps) runs on it natively. It also streams from a PC if you have one.

The weak spot is the LCD: blacks are dark grey, so night scenes look flatter than on the micro-OLED headsets. A better head strap is the single best upgrade for long sessions.

Meta Quest 3S Best on a budget

Same chip and the same apps as the Quest 3 for much less money. The savings come from the lenses and screen: older Fresnel lenses with a smaller sweet spot, a lower resolution, and a narrower 96° view. VR video still looks good, and 8K files are already more than it can show, so there is no point paying extra for anything higher.

Valve Steam Frame For PC power users

Valve's first standalone headset, released in September 2026. It runs SteamOS and can play some apps on its own, but its real strength is streaming from a gaming PC. It ships with its own Wi-Fi 6E adapter for a dedicated wireless link, and uses eye tracking to send the sharpest part of the stream exactly where you are looking. That makes it one of the best ways to watch very high-bitrate files from a PC without a cable.

Two things to know. The passthrough cameras are black and white (a colour add-on exists), and its list of apps that run on the headset itself is still growing, so check your favourite player is supported before you rely on it standalone.

Apple Vision Pro (M5)

Apple's 2025 refresh with the M5 chip. A stunning micro-OLED display (23 million pixels across both eyes, up to 120 Hz) and the best eye and hand tracking around. It is also the heaviest headset here and by far the most expensive, and Apple's store is strict about adult content, so VR video playback depends on third-party players and files you load yourself.

Bigscreen Beyond 2 and 2e Sharpest picture

The opposite of the all-in-one headsets: a 107-gram visor that does nothing but show you a picture, made to fit your face exactly. It needs a capable gaming PC, a cable, and at least one SteamVR base station (sold separately), so it is a project rather than a gadget. In return you get micro-OLED at 2560 × 2560 per eye, a 108° view and roughly 32 pixels per degree: the best VR video picture you can buy today, with almost no weight on your face.

Before you buy

  • Check the app first. On a new platform, the headset is only as good as the VR video player that runs on it.
  • Download beats streaming for the sharpest picture. Streams are compressed harder than downloads, whatever the headset.
  • Glasses wearers: Quest 3 and Steam Frame have room for your glasses. The slim visors, Bigscreen Beyond and the Meta VR Glasses, use prescription lens inserts instead.
  • Prices move. Check the maker's store before you decide; the figures here are from the date at the top.
